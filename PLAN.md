    **The bank's in-app transaction lists are deliberately out of scope**, and that is a decision rather than an omission. Two more screenshot kinds exist — SCB EASY's deposit inquiry and Krungthai Connext's account detail — and both are month-long lists rather than single transactions. Neither prints a per-row balance and neither carries a totals block, so there is **nothing to cross-check a misread row against**: the two guarantees that make a statement importable (`confirm_import`'s balance walk and D-033's printed-totals check) both have nothing to work with, and a dropped or misread row would fail *open*. Krungthai's list is worse still — its rows carry no year at all, only the month tab does. Those transactions arrive as a real statement anyway, which is already imported.

    **Four problems, and three of them already have their answer in this repository.**

    - **No reference number on any card**, which is what `public.slips` is built around: `slip_reference text not null` with `unique (owner_id, bank_code, slip_reference)` is what makes re-sharing a slip a no-op, and the QR's CRC is what makes that key trustworthy. A card has neither. **The precedent is the statement importer**, which faces exactly this — a statement row carries no bank-issued id either — and answers it with `private.row_fingerprint` over `private.normalize_source_text`. A computed fingerprint over bank, account last four, amount, timestamp and balance is the same shape. **Record what it is not**: a fingerprint is idempotent, not tamper-evident, which is strictly weaker than what a CRC-protected reference gives a slip.
    - **Two of the three print a two-digit Buddhist year**, and `readPrintedDate` refuses that today. **The machinery to resolve it already exists and is already proven**: `resolveStatementEra` in `lib/dates.ts` reads the year in both calendars and keeps whichever falls inside a plausible window, and because the two readings are always 543 years apart a window narrower than that admits exactly one — a test walks all 100 two-digit years and shows the ambiguous branch unreachable. A card is days old, so its window is far tighter than a statement's 20-years-back. What `readPrintedDate` lacks is not arithmetic but an anchor: a slip has no period-end frame, and a card's anchor is its capture time. **The era rule must be per-layout, never global** — SCB's cards are four-digit Gregorian while the other two are two-digit Buddhist, and applying one rule to both reproduces D-031, where `2500 + 26 − 543 = 1983` dated a whole statement 43 years early and parsed cleanly the entire way.
    - **The balance is the ledger balance — measured 2026-08-12, and it is exact.** This was the owner's own answer on 2026-08-11 and it is now evidence rather than a claim. **All six cards matched, 6 of 6, across all three layouts**: for each one a transaction exists in the hosted ledger at the same account, the same date **and the same time**, carrying a balance equal to the card's to the satang. Not close — equal. That makes it the most valuable field on the card and it should be stored and used, for three separate jobs: it **breaks the tie the amount cannot**, since D-063 measured 16.3% of rows sharing a bank and exact amount with another within three days and the rule currently refuses that coin toss outright, while two same-amount rows never share a running balance; it is a **fail-closed cross-check**, because a card matching on amount and date whose balance contradicts the row should refuse rather than pair; and it strengthens the fingerprint above. **A second finding came free and strengthens the fingerprint further:** the card's printed timestamp equals the statement row's on all six, so account plus date plus time already locates the row and the balance then confirms it. **Limits worth keeping**: n is 6 across 2 cards per layout, both cards in each screenshot share an account and a day, and none was captured while a hold was outstanding — two layouts label the field *available* balance and the third *remaining* balance, which diverge at many banks exactly when a hold exists. So the balance is proven for the ordinary case, which is enough to make it a tie-breaker and a cross-check and **not** enough to make it identity.
    - **Account matching must be per-layout, and assuming otherwise fails silently.** Found on 2026-08-12 by the owner, after a first pass of this measurement wrongly reported the two KBank cards as unmatchable. SCB Connect and Krungthai Connext both print the **last four** digits, which is what `public.accounts.last_four` stores, so those match directly. **KBank Live does not**: it formats the account as `xxx-x-xxxxx-x` and reveals digits **6–9** while masking the final one, so its visible group overlaps the stored last four by three digits and sits one digit off. Matching a KBank card on `last_four` therefore hits nothing, on every card, forever — and it fails as *no such account* rather than as an error, which is the worst available shape. Once matched on the correct account both KBank cards agreed on timestamp and balance like the other four. **This is the same lesson as the era rule below**: one layout's convention applied globally reproduces D-031, where a single wrong assumption dated an entire statement 43 years early and parsed cleanly the whole way.
    - ~~**It needs its own table**, so the backup goes **v5 → v6 with 2, 3, 4 and 5 staying restorable forever**, with new pgTAP contracts and a new restore version pair to prove.~~ **Done 2026-08-12** (D-098, migration 016): the table, 26 pgTAP contracts, and the v5-into-v6 pair run end to end into a separately bound project. A card is not a slip (no reference, no QR payload, and `slips_immutable` forbids retrofitting one) and not a cash entry (cash is never matched, and a card certainly will be). **The fingerprint carries the balance, and that is the load-bearing part** — without it two payments of the same amount at the same minute are one row, proven by removing it.

    **What carries over unchanged**, which is most of the surface: the capture form and its confirm-the-amount posture (task 20), the OCR amount finder and D-087's rule that the owner reads the figure rather than the machine storing it (task 21), the abbreviated Thai month table measured across all three layouts (D-086), and the whole reconciliation path that collapses a captured payment onto its statement row (task 22). **OCR should also work better here than it does on slips** — these are screenshots of rendered text at native resolution rather than photographs of paper — but that changes the odds, not the rule: D-087's argument was about consequence, since a wrong figure arrives wearing the same confidence as a right one.

29. **A card's correction overlay, its stored match decision, and a way to retire one.** Scoped 2026-08-13 (D-103) and **DONE 2026-08-14** (D-104, migration 017). Held back from task 27 on purpose: each piece needs its own table and therefore its own backup version bump, so they were designed together and landed in **one migration (017), moving the backup v6 → v7** with v2 … v6 staying restorable. This is D-098's argument one version later, and D-089's: the v6-into-v7 restore pair was proven end to end *before* anything depends on it. The scope below is what was built, with two things worth reading as outcomes rather than plans — the acknowledgement is **stored consent** rather than a computed flag, and the ledger grew a **retired-cards list** so that reversibility is real on screen and not only in the database.

    **The third piece was not in the original scope and the ledger view is what surfaced it.** Nothing in any migration can retire any record — no slip, no cash entry and no card can be withdrawn once captured, so every one counts in the totals forever. That rarely bites a slip, whose identity comes from a CRC-checked QR. A card is typed from a screenshot, so a wrong account binding or a second capture with a typo is a likelier accident, and until now it would sit in the ledger permanently.

    **Four tables, following the slip precedents rather than inventing a shape.** `notification_card_corrections` and its append-only revisions table copy `slip_correction_overlays` (migration 013): one nullable column per correctable field, where null means *not corrected*. `notification_card_decisions` and its revisions table copy `slip_match_overlays` (migration 012): a decision, an optimistic `revision`, and a **partial unique index of its own** so one transaction may be claimed by at most one card — deliberately not shared with the slip index, because a row may legitimately carry both records (D-102).

    **What is correctable and what is not.** Correctable: `kind`, `amount_minor`, `balance_minor`, `occurred_on`, `occurred_at_time`, `counterparty`, `category_id`, `note` — every figure the owner typed. **Not correctable: `account_id`, `channel` and `printed_account_digits`.** The binding was checked against the printed digits under that layout's mask at capture and cannot be re-made (D-101 says so in a message the form already shows); the channel and the digits are what that check was made against, so all three travel together. The remedy for a wrong binding is retirement, below. `balance_minor` carries no sign check for the reason `lib/notification-cards.ts` already gives — a balance is a position, zero is ordinary and negative is an overdraft.

    **The decision has three values, not two.** `matched` names a statement row; `unmatched` says this card is on none of them; **`not-a-payment` retires it** — the card leaves the ledger rows and the totals while staying in its append-only table, because nothing here is ever deleted. Folding retirement into the decision rather than giving it a fourth table keeps one place answering "what is this card's relationship to the ledger". **Retirement is reversible and the card row is not**: a decision is an overlay with a revision, so a card retired by mistake is un-retired by changing the decision.

    **A balance disagreement may be overruled, and the overrule is recorded** (owner's decision, D-103). The RPC compares the card's **corrected** balance with the row's printed balance and **refuses by default**, which is the fail-closed posture D-102 built. The refusal lifts only when the request carries an explicit acknowledgement, and that acknowledgement is what gets stored — `accepted_balance_mismatch`. Storing the consent rather than a computed flag is what keeps it from going stale when the balance is later corrected. The RPC still re-checks the account and the exact amount server-side, exactly as `set_slip_match` re-checks bank and amount.

    **Two read-side rules that are requirements rather than choices.** Corrections are applied **before** the rule, by a `cardsInForce` mirroring `slipsInForce` — migration 014 exists because `set_slip_match` compared a slip's *original* amount, and a card has two figures with that trap instead of one, since it reconciles on the balance as well. And decisions are applied **before** the rule, never layered over its result, so an automatic pairing can never consume a row the owner already assigned (`proposeSlipMatches` records why).

    **Re-capturing a retired card needs no new mechanism, and this is worth not re-deriving.** The fingerprint covers the account, so capturing the same screenshot against the *right* account yields a different fingerprint and therefore a new row. Capturing the identical thing again hits the retired row and returns `captured: false` as it does today; the route says it is already held and retired, and the owner un-retires it by changing the decision.

    **What this does not include.** No new route beyond the two the decisions and corrections need, and no change to `capture_notification_card`. Task 27 is complete and this does not re-open it.
